THE BAR
A defensible justification for adding one feature to an agent system

Correction, 2026-09-20: this document originally overstated what its sources establish. The guidance below now distinguishes planning power from an observed-effect threshold, inconclusive results from equivalence, and a local repeatability check from a reusable noise floor. Microsoft feature-success rates are context, not a measured prior for prompt rules. Fractional-factorial claims require the actual alias structure. These corrections do not change historical run results or retroactively change their decision rules.

This is a gate, not a guide. Walk it in order. A proposed feature clears the bar only if every requirement in parts 1 to 4 is either satisfied or explicitly waived in writing with a reason. Each requirement names the source it comes from. Part 5 lists the requirements that no source supports; those are labelled ASSUMPTION and are ours, not borrowed.

PART 0. THE PRIOR YOU ARGUE AGAINST

0.1 Treat intuition as a hypothesis. Across well-designed controlled experiments on Microsoft online properties, about one third improved their intended key metric, about one third were flat, and about one third hurt it (Kohavi, Crook, Longbotham et al., Online Experimentation at Microsoft, 2009; restated in KDD 2013). These were web-product experiments, not prompt rules. Selection into experimentation also matters. The result motivates measuring our changes; it does not establish that two thirds of this skill's rules are useless or harmful. Any numeric transfer is the unvalidated assumption in A5.1, not a finding. [Original paper](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExPThinkWeek2009Public.pdf).

0.2 Deployment rate is a related but different outcome. At Bing, 33.4% of experiment groups were ultimately deployed to all users, across 21,220 experiments in 19 components over roughly two and a half years, with an average of 42 days and 1.8 iterations per experiment (Kevic, Murphy, Williams, Beckmann, Characterizing Experimentation in Continuous Deployment: a Case Study on Bing, 2017). This is not an independent estimate of the probability that a prompt rule improves agent performance, nor is deployment identical to a statistically demonstrated metric improvement.

0.3 Size your expectations to the effect distribution. Rule #2 of Kohavi, Deng, Longbotham and Xu (Seven Rules of Thumb for Web Site Experimenters, KDD 2014) is that changes rarely have a big positive impact: the "success rate on ideas at Bing is about 10-20%, in line with other search engines", and those that succeed "improve key metrics by 0.1% to 1.0%, once diluted to overall impact". The more the system has already been tuned, the lower the hit rate on the next addition. The same paper notes that "following taillights is easier than innovating in isolation", so importing a feature that another team has already tested carries a higher prior than inventing one.

0.4 For agent systems specifically, do not assume that more instruction, more effort or more scaffolding helps. In the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (Princeton PLI, ICLR 2026; 21,730 rollouts, 9 models by 9 benchmarks, about $40,000 of spend, 2.5B tokens of logs), "For 21 of 36 runs, higher reasoning effort does not improve accuracy." Narang et al. (Do Transformer Modifications Transfer Across Implementations and Applications?, EMNLP 2021) reached the same shape of conclusion for architecture: "most modifications do not meaningfully improve performance".

PART 1. WHAT MUST BE FIXED BEFORE ANY RUN HAPPENS

1.1 One Overall Evaluation Criterion, chosen and written down before execution. The five-phase continuous experimentation process synthesised by Auer, Ros, Kaltenbrunner, Runeson and Felderer (Controlled Experimentation in Continuous Experimentation, 2021) requires the OEC to be selected at design time as "the most important metric for deciding on the outcome of the experiment". One metric, not a basket you interpret afterwards.

1.2 The OEC must survive an adversarial check. Kohavi, Deng, Frasca, Longbotham, Walker and Xu (Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: Five Puzzling Outcomes Explained, KDD 2012) name as the first pitfall "Picking an OEC for which it is easy to beat the control by doing something clearly wrong from a business perspective", and demonstrate it with a Bing quality bug where degraded results pushed "distinct queries per user up over 10%, and revenue per user up over 30%". Write down the dumbest change that would maximise your candidate metric. If that change is obviously bad, the metric is not an OEC yet. For an agent, metrics such as tasks attempted, tool calls made or steps taken fail this check directly, because a feature that makes the agent thrash and retry scores as an improvement.

1.3 Guardrail metrics that must not degrade, named alongside the OEC. Cost is one of them and is a top-line number, not a footnote: Kapoor, Stroebl, Siegel, Nadgir and Narayanan (AI Agents That Matter, 2024) require accuracy to be plotted against inference cost, and report that Reflexion and LDB cost over 50% more than a trivial warming baseline for no significant accuracy gain, while LATS cost over 50 times more (five runs per agent, HumanEval, 164 problems).

1.4 The expected behaviour and the gap it is meant to close, written before implementation. The HYPEX model (Hypothesis Experiment Data-Driven Development, Olsson and Bosch, as described in Mattos, Towards Automated Experiments in Software Intensive Systems, 2018) requires "feature prioritization and gap specification" before a minimum viable feature is built, and comparison of "the actual behavior with the expected one" after.

1.5 Record the count of candidate features being screened and any assumptions about their success rate. Kohavi et al. (KDD 2014) give (1-beta)·pi / [(1-beta)·pi + alpha·(1-pi)] for the probability that a significant result is a true positive under the stated prior and power model. At alpha = 0.05 and beta = 0.20, pi = 1/3 gives 89%; pi = 1/500 gives 3.1%. These are conditional examples, not calibrated posteriors for this programme. If a numeric prior is used, justify it or report sensitivity to plausible values; do not borrow Microsoft's rate as a fact about prompt rules. Account for multiple candidate tests and seek confirmation on untouched tasks.

1.6 Plan sensitivity for the chosen estimand and test before the confirmatory run. Miller (Adding Error Bars to Evals, Anthropic, 2024) explains how sample size, effect size, repeated sampling and question variability affect power. His example needs about 969 questions to detect a three-percentage-point difference under its stated assumptions at 80% power and alpha 0.05; this is not a universal minimum for every agent experiment. Kohavi (KDD 2015) describes the approximate variance-over-effect-squared scaling: halving the targeted effect can quadruple the required observations under the same model. Task clustering and skewed resource metrics can change the appropriate uncertainty calculation. Record the practically worthwhile effect, alpha, target power, sampling assumptions and method, and label estimates derived retrospectively as such. A paired-mean approximation must not be reported as the sensitivity of a sign test, and an MDE is not a hard detection floor.

1.7 Estimate repeatability when it informs the comparison. Henderson, Islam, Bachman, Pineau, Precup and Meger (Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters, 2017) took 10 trials of one algorithm with identical hyperparameters on HalfCheetah, split them arbitrarily into two groups of five, and obtained a statistically significant difference between the two halves (t = -9.0916, p = 0.0016). They decline to name a fixed trial count and point to power analysis instead. An A/A check can diagnose variability for the sampled tasks, model, harness, budget and metric; it is not a universal noise floor, a mandatory gate supplied by that paper, or a replacement for uncertainty on the actual treatment contrast.

1.8 A stopping rule fixed before the first run, in one of three legal forms. Fix the sample size in advance and test significance exactly once; or use always-valid p-values and confidence sequences, which Johari, Pekelis and Walsh (Always Valid Inference, 2015/2022) introduced precisely because "A/B tests are typically analyzed via frequentist p-values and confidence intervals; but these inferences are wholly unreliable if users endogenously choose samples sizes by continuously monitoring their tests"; or declare a maximum information total and an alpha spending function, after which, per DeMets and Lan (Interim Analysis: The Alpha Spending Function Approach, 1994), "neither the number of interim analyses nor the calender times (or information fractions) need to be specified in advance. Only the spending function must be determined." Watching results and calling it when one arm pulls ahead is not a stopping rule. Miller (How Not To Run An A/B Test, 2010) quantifies what it costs: testing after every observation with a 50% baseline, no true effect, stopping at p < 0.05 or abandoning at 150 observations, gives a false-positive rate of "26.1% - more than five times what you probably thought the significance level was". His correction table for holding a true 5% is 2.9% for one extra look, 2.2% for two, 1.8% for three, 1.4% for five and 1.0% for ten.

1.9 A futility threshold, written as a number, with the effect size it is computed against. He, Lai and Liao (Futility stopping in clinical trials, 2012) note that "Although stopping early for futility would not increase the type I error, it would decrease the power of the test", and warn that the common shortcut of stopping when the point estimate lands on the null "ignores the uncertainty in the estimate and can lose substantial power due to premature stopping". Abandoning a candidate early costs no false-positive budget, which makes it the cheapest move available; it is only well defined once the implied alternative is named.

1.10 A validated grader, written adversarially, before the feature is measured. The Agentic Benchmark Checklist (Zhu, Jin, Pruksachatkun et al., Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks, NeurIPS 2025) separates task validity from outcome validity and reports that "SWE-bench Verified uses insufficient test cases, while TAU-bench counts empty responses as successful. Such issues can lead to under- or overestimation of agents' performance by up to 100% in relative terms", with ABC reducing performance overestimation on CVE-Bench by 33%. If the success criterion can be met without the behaviour the feature produces, the measured delta means nothing.

1.11 A holdout matched to the generality being claimed, kept from the feature's author. Kapoor et al. (2024) require in-distribution holdout for a distribution-specific claim, out-of-distribution samples for a task-specific claim, unseen tasks for a domain-general claim and unseen domains for a fully general claim, and found appropriate holdouts in only 5 of 17 agent benchmarks surveyed (1/1 distribution-specific, 3/6 task-specific, 1/8 domain-general, 0/2 fully general). Tuning a feature against the same example runs that motivated it is the shortcut failure this is designed to catch.

1.12 If several features are tested together, choose a design for the effects to be estimated and record its assumptions. NIST/SEMATECH 5.3.3.4.4 distinguishes resolution III, where main effects can alias with two-factor interactions; resolution IV, where main effects are clear of two-factor interactions but some pairs of interactions alias; and resolution V, which separates all main effects and two-factor interactions from one another while retaining aliasing with higher-order terms. A particular interaction can sometimes be clear in a lower-resolution design: inspect the actual alias structure rather than treating the resolution label as sufficient. The four-factor half-fraction used as an example here cannot isolate any individual two-factor interaction; see 4.2. Screening designs such as Plackett-Burman trade attribution for fewer configurations, and definitive screening designs have their own factor-level and model assumptions. None is automatically suitable for dependent prompt clauses, invalid feature combinations or an uncalibrated task set. [NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri3344.htm).

1.13 A run budget that is deliberately under-spent. NIST 5.3.3: "It is a good idea to choose a design that requires somewhat fewer runs than the budget permits, so that center point runs can be added to check for curvature in a 2-level screening design and backup resources are available to redo runs that have processing mishaps." Reserve runs for repeats of the same configuration and for re-running configurations that failed for infrastructure reasons.

1.14 The ceremony tier, declared before the work, not after the result. Kevic et al. (2017) found that "Requiring a hypothesis for every change is too great an overhead for small changes, such as big fixes", and that changes linked to experiments were much larger than others (78.9 files for matched changes, 122.2 for high-probability, against 11.7 for other code changes). A feature intended to change agent behaviour measurably gets the full gate; a wording correction gets a lighter check. The tier must be fixed in advance so it cannot be used as an escape hatch.

1.15 A removal mechanism installed at add time. Mahdavi-Hezaveh, Dremann and Williams (Software Development with Feature Toggles, 2019/2021; 17 practices from 99 grey-literature artifacts, 10 peer-reviewed papers and artifacts from 38 companies, with 20 survey responses from 45 companies) record that the working mechanism is enforced rather than intended: "If the toggle exists after its expiration date, a test fails or the application does not start, which causes a developer to remove the toggle." They also record why the soft version fails, in a practitioner's words: "the clean-up ticket is just at the very top of the next Sprint's backlog for like six months." A promise to review later is the exact mechanism observed to fail.

PART 2. WHAT THE EXPERIMENT ITSELF MUST SATISFY

2.1 Matched budget. Narang et al. (2021): "For each architectural modification, we attempt to keep either the parameter count or total operations in the model approximately the same to perform a fair comparison with the baseline model." A feature that consumes context or tokens must beat a baseline allowed to spend the same amount elsewhere.

2.2 A second implementation context. Narang et al. re-tested published modifications in a codebase other than the one each was born in, across several downstream tasks, and found most did not transfer. A feature that only helps in the setup that produced it is an implementation artefact until shown otherwise.

2.3 Equal tuning effort on both arms. Melis, Dyer and Blunsom (On the State of the Art of Evaluation in Neural Language Models, 2017) identify "differing code bases and limited computational resources, which represent uncontrolled sources of experimental variation", and show that giving every arm the same automatic search budget reversed the published ranking. The analogue here is wording, ordering and placement: if you polish the new feature's prompt text but not the baseline's, this is the standing objection to your result. Either equalise the editing effort or fix it explicitly and say so.

2.4 A tuned simple baseline, and the trivial baselines. Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach (Are We Really Making Much Progress?, RecSys 2019) reproduced 18 neural recommendation papers from top venues: "Only 7 of them could be reproduced with reasonable effort", and 6 of those 7 were often beaten by comparably simple heuristics, with weak or untuned baselines named as the mechanism. For agents, Kapoor et al. add the specific comparators: retry, warming and escalation. They report that "'State-of-the-art' agent architectures for HumanEval do not outperform simple baselines. There is no significant accuracy difference between our warming strategy and the best-performing agent architecture." The comparator for "add this feature" is not "no feature", it is "spend those tokens on a retry, or on nothing".

2.5 Enough trials, with confidence intervals, and no selection of top runs. Henderson et al.: "many trials must be run with different random seeds when comparing performance."

2.6 Randomise the nuisance sources rather than freezing them. Bouthillier, Delaunay, Bronzi et al. (Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks, MLSys 2021) measured on five tasks and architectures that "adding more sources of variation to an imperfect estimator approaches better the ideal estimator at a 51 times reduction in compute cost". Vary task instances, ordering and session context across runs and judge the feature on the distribution. A feature that only wins under one frozen configuration is what this estimator is built to catch.

2.7 No oracle stopping for any self-correction, reflection or critique feature. Huang, Chen, Mishra et al. (Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet, ICLR 2024) found that "LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction". If the harness stops revising once the answer is right, you have measured oracle-guided filtering. Use a fixed protocol with no label access and report both directions: how often the feature rescues a bad run and how often it spoils a good one.

2.8 Cost measured on the same axes as the outcome, with the trivial baselines on the same plot, and a win declared only on the Pareto frontier (Kapoor et al., 2024).

2.9 More than one model. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard reports that "The Pareto frontier of accuracy and cost is often steep" and that "In only 1 of 9 benchmarks do we observe the most costly model run on the frontier". A feature that helps a weak model can be inert or harmful on a strong one, so a single-model result does not support a general claim.

2.10 Peeking confined to the rule declared in 1.8, with the number of looks counted as they happen.

PART 3. WHAT MUST BE TRUE BEFORE THE FEATURE SHIPS

3.1 Assess the pre-specified estimand against a separately declared practically worthwhile effect, using the chosen interval, test and stopping rule. An MDE is the effect size a design detects with the specified power under its assumptions; it is not a hard detection floor or a required observed-effect threshold. Power calculations must match the analysis: a paired-mean approximation does not establish power for a sign test. Report any posterior from 1.5 with its assumptions, not as a substitute for the observed estimate and uncertainty.

3.2 Assess guardrails and the accuracy-cost tradeoff with uncertainty (Kapoor et al., 2024). If claiming that a cheaper configuration preserves quality, use a pre-specified non-inferiority or equivalence margin and an analysis that supports that claim. Overlapping confidence intervals or a non-significant difference alone do not establish equal outcomes or no degradation.

3.3 The passing transcripts were read, not counted. Aleithan, Xue, Mohajer et al. (SWE-Bench+, 2024) manually screened the successful patches of the then leading SWE-Agent plus GPT-4 on SWE-bench (2,294 issues, 12 Python repositories, over 94% predating the model's cutoff) and found "32.67% of the successful patches involve cheating as the solutions were directly provided in the issue report or the comments ... 31.08% of the passed patches are suspicious patches due to weak test cases ... the resolution rate of SWE-Agent+GPT-4 dropped from 12.47% to 3.97%." Credit the feature only for passes where the transcript shows its behaviour mattered.

3.4 Any large or surprising result was investigated as a defect first. Kohavi and Longbotham (Unexpected Results in Online Controlled Experiments, 2010) open with Twyman's law, "Any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong", note that "if a result is truly unexpected, it's probably wrong", and close with "Getting numbers is easy, getting numbers you can trust is quite difficult." The same paper records the asymmetry to guard against: people resist and question negative results while accepting positive ones, so the investigation trigger is the size and surprise of the effect, not its sign. Check for eval contamination, a changed grader, and silently dropped task subsets.

3.5 Separate a product decision from the strength of the inference. The programme may decline an addition whose benefit is unproven and whose cost is material. A non-significant difference, however, does not demonstrate no effect. Evidence of practical equivalence requires a pre-specified margin and an interval or test that supports it. Retaining or dropping a feature under uncertainty must be recorded as a decision under uncertainty, not proof that the feature does nothing.

3.6 Per-feature evidence, with no bundling, and with evidence beyond the ablation table. Lipton and Steinhardt (Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship, 2018) write that "Too frequently, authors propose many tweaks absent proper ablation studies, obscuring the source of empirical gains", and also that "ablation is neither necessary nor sufficient for understanding a method, and can even be impractical given computational constraints". The defensible position is to ablate the feature under debate and back it with error analysis on the transcripts where the feature was supposed to fire.

3.7 The verdict is one of exactly three words. Kevic et al.: "If the overall evaluation criterion (OEC) measurably improved with the new version of the product, then the treatment of the experiment is shipped and abandoned in the contrary." HYPEX closes each round by "deciding if the feature should be abandoned, iterated once more, or if it should be considered completed", and the five-phase process allows only persevere or pivot on an unsatisfactory result. Iterate is legal when the metric was wrong or the sample was too small. Liking the feature is not a reason to iterate, and "leave it in and move on" is not one of the allowed verdicts.

3.8 The generality claimed in the shipping note is the generality the holdout of 1.11 supports, and no more.

PART 4. WHAT MUST BE RECORDED, WHATEVER THE RESULT

The record is written for every candidate, including every one that is dropped, and it contains at minimum the following.

4.1 The pre-registration block from part 1, reproduced as written before the run: OEC and estimand, guardrails, expected behaviour and gap, any prior assumptions, number of candidates screened, planning MDE, any local repeatability evidence, stopping rule, futility threshold, tier.

4.2 Record the design and its estimable contrasts. For a fractional factorial, give its resolution and alias structure (NIST 5.3.3.4.4); factorial notation is not required for designs that are not fractional factorials. In the four-factor, eight-configuration resolution IV design with I = ABCD, main effects are clear of two-factor interactions but aliased with three-factor interactions, while AB = CD, AC = BD and AD = BC. An observed interaction contrast cannot identify one of those pairs without additional assumptions or follow-up runs. Other resolution IV designs can have particular unaliased pairs, so check the actual design. [NIST](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri3344.htm).

4.3 Record the planning MDE with its target power, alpha, estimand, variance assumptions and task population, plus any local repeatability evidence. A non-significant result is inconclusive at the reported precision; an effect below the planning MDE remains detectable with lower probability. Use the interval to state which practically relevant effects remain compatible with the data (Miller 2024; Kohavi et al., Rule #7).

4.4 The number of looks actually taken and the stopping rule actually used, including any deviation (Miller 2010; Johari et al.; DeMets and Lan). DeMets and Lan also bound the abuse: Proschan et al. "showed that the a level can be doubled if one tries their best to abuse the use of a spending function", though with common spending functions deliberate trend-chasing "did not inflate the a level more than could reasonably occur by accident".

4.5 If a posterior probability is reported, include the prior, likelihood or power assumptions and sensitivity analysis; the illustrative calculation in 1.5 is not a calibrated probability for this programme (Kohavi et al., KDD 2014).

4.6 The cost delta in tokens, latency and money, alongside the outcome delta (Kapoor et al., 2024).

4.7 The decision, as one of ship, abandon or iterate, with the reason (Kevic et al.; HYPEX). Separately state whether the evidence supports benefit, harm, practical equivalence within a declared margin, or remains inconclusive. Abandoning an inconclusive candidate does not establish no effect.

4.8 The transcript evidence supporting the verdict, including the passes inspected and any leakage or weak-grader findings (SWE-Bench+; ABC).

4.9 For anything shipped, the expiration date and the enforcing mechanism from 1.15, named concretely (Mahdavi-Hezaveh et al.).

4.10 An explicit statement of where validity could not be guaranteed. ABC requires reporting explicitly where task validity or outcome validity cannot be established. Anything skipped is recorded as skipped.

PART 5. ASSUMPTIONS (no source behind these; they are ours)

A5.1 Treating a rule in an agent skill package, a prompt clause, a scaffolding step and a tool as the same unit of intervention is unvalidated. The sources measure web product features, model architecture modifications and agent scaffolds, not prompt-level rules. Web-feature success rates must not be presented as an empirical prior for the 188 historical rules; dependencies and alternative ways of splitting rules also make that count unsuitable as a count of independent trials.

A5.2 The specific numeric thresholds. A futility kill at conditional power below 20% after a stated fraction of the budget, a declared maximum of N pairs before the first run, and any particular alpha spending shape are choices we make; the sources supply the method and the formula, not our numbers.

A5.3 A/A checks are a local diagnostic choice. Henderson et al. demonstrate that identical configurations can split significantly; they do not prescribe an A/A gate or a reusable threshold. Reconsider variance estimates when tasks, models, harnesses or budgets change.

A5.4 The tier definitions in 1.14. Kevic et al. show that Bing reserved the experimentation apparatus for larger changes and give file-count evidence; where exactly the line falls for agent features is invented here.

A5.5 Keeping a written record for dropped candidates, and reading that record before proposing a similar feature. HYPEX and the five-phase model require a verdict; neither requires an archive or a duplicate check.

A5.6 Treating context and token budget as the direct analogue of the deployment cost that makes a flat result a stop. The 2009 paper's "cost to additional deployments" is about deployment and maintenance, not context windows.

A5.7 Restructuring task sets so that each run contributes many scored items rather than one, in order to approach Miller's sample-size requirement. That is a consequence we draw; he prescribes eval size, not run design.

A5.8 The ordering of this document as a gate, with part 1 blocking part 2 and part 2 blocking part 3. The sources supply the individual requirements; the sequencing and the veto structure are ours.
